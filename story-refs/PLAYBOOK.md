# "In This Town" story playbook

**Daily task: read this before writing any story.** It sums up the patterns in 14 popular "In this town" Shorts (1M–14M views) that Harshit picked as references. Use the *structure* only. Never reuse their premises, characters, twists or lines; every HarShitified story is original. Format notes for each reference are in `refs.md`.

## Channel direction: HORROR, OPEN-ENDED (Harshit, 2026-11-26)
Most Shorts are now **horror** in the "in this town" format, and most end **open**. This overrides the "end warm or funny" pattern below.
- **Mix:** roughly 3 of every 4 Shorts are horror (creature horror, uncanny/psychological "something is wrong with everyone", folk horror rules and rituals, liminal/empty-place dread). At most 1 in 4 is darkly funny, and it should still be creepy.
- **Tone:** dread over jokes. Keep humour to one or two dry lines early on (in the town details), then let the fear build. Slow down near the end: quieter lines, longer pauses, darker light.
- **The twist still lands clearly:** the viewer must understand what the narrator discovered (the rule was wrong, the thing was never what the town thought, it was already inside). What stays open is what happens NEXT.
- **Open endings (pick one, vary them):** the threat is still out there and the narrator knows it; a final detail shows it isn't over (one more knock, a second set of footprints, the rule quietly changes); the narrator realises something about themselves or someone they love and stops talking; a question to the viewer that can't be answered ("So if you hear it tonight... do you open the door?"); or a cut mid-sentence that loops back to the first line. No neat explanation, no "and we all lived happily". The viewer should finish wanting to comment their theory.
- **Still never gory:** no blood, wounds or on-screen deaths, and nothing that shows harm done to a child. People are "taken", "gone", "never came back"; the horror comes from what you don't see, sound, silence, wrong faces, and things standing where they shouldn't.
- **Look:** lean on night streets, dark forests, fog, empty houses and lit windows, with a creature or wrong-looking person kept partly hidden (silhouette, glowing eyes, too-tall shapes in the distance) rather than shown in full.

## The 9-beat structure (our target: 95-115 s, anything under 2 minutes; times below are rough)

| Beat | Time | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| 1. The rule | 0-3 s | First words: **"In this town, …"** plus ONE impossible law, and its consequence in the same breath ("…and if you do X, Y happens"). No name or setup before it. |
| 2. Proof of the rule | 3-10 s | 2-4 quick, specific, *funny-weird* details of daily life under the rule (pets in socks, a whispered school bell, a mayor who has broken the rule 4,000 times). This is where a town comes alive. Keep each detail to one short sentence and one shot. |
| 3. The legend | optional, 1 line | Someone who tried before and failed ("He made it to step 998"). It shows the stakes are real. |
| 4. The outsider | 10-15 s | The kid narrator, **always first person, "I"** ("I had just moved here…", "My name is ___"). They are new in town and nobody told them the rule, OR they are the one person who doesn't fit (the loudest kid in the quiet town, the careful kid in the fearless town). Give them **one small habit or trait**; it pays off later. |
| 5. Someone they love | 15-20 s | A little sister, a best friend or a grandpa: the person at risk. Stakes are always personal. |
| 6. The rule breaks | 20-30 s | The rule is broken, by accident or for love. Something big and visual happens at once (the ground cracks, every door flies open, a creature appears). |
| 7. Escalation by counting | 30-45 s | Raise the pressure in numbered steps: "Step 100… Step 600… Step 997", "Day three… day five… day six", "10 steps… 20… 30". It's easy to follow and builds tension fast. |
| 8. The reframe twist | 45-55 s | The town had the rule wrong. What everyone feared was really something else (the monster was lonely, not hungry; everyone had the same secret). **The small habit from beat 4 is what saves them.** |
| 9. The button | last 5-8 s | Now usually an OPEN, creepy last beat (see Channel direction above); a funny one only for the occasional darkly funny Short: the grumpy adult gets a comeuppance, or "the next morning, one more card fell from the sky…". OR cut off the last word mid-sentence so the video loops back to the start. |

## Why the biggest ones won (8M-14M views)
- **A full arc with heart.** The top five all end warm or funny, not just scary. Horror gets people watching; family or friendship gets the shares.
- **Setup → payoff.** A tiny detail early on (saying good night to the house, answering "five") turns out to be the solution. Viewers rewatch to catch it.
- **Comedy inside the horror.** About one joke every 10 seconds, often about the mayor, a parent, or a pet.
- **A visual, simple rule** a kid could explain: the rule shows up as something (a card, a mouse, paint, a remote).

The short horror-only ones (35-55 s, 1M-3M views) still work. Their recipe: rule → break → monster → a one-line twist ("Welcome home, master") → cut off.

## Writing rules
- **First person, always.** The lead tells their own story as "I" ("My little sister…", "Nobody told me the rule"). Never "a boy named ___". The narrator may give their own name once; everyone else gets a short invented name.
- Short sentences, everyday words, past tense.
- Sound words land on cuts: *Poof. Boom. Splash.* Short dialogue lines in quotes.
- Names: short and plain (Pip, Leo, Max, Mia), but **change them every day**. Check `_published.md` and don't repeat a name from the last 14 videos.
- The mayor or a greedy grown-up makes a good recurring joke target.
- Spooky, never gory. Nobody dies on screen; "taken", "gone" and "never came back" are enough.
- Never claim it's real. Use no "#roblox" tags or game names; our characters are original Block Town blockies.

## Fit to our channel rules
Length: **under 2 minutes, usually 95-115 s** (about 260-340 words at our natural 1.0 voice speed). Harshit's rule (2026-11-26): never rush a story to fit a time limit. Give the build-up room: let the town details land, let the reader feel the stakes for the person the narrator loves, and give each counted escalation step its own moment. Pacing: one sentence per line where possible, a real pause after every reveal, scene change and line of dialogue (dialogue gets a breath before and after so it never starts or ends abruptly), and a slower final beat. Rough split: beats 1-2 ≈ 15 s, 3-5 ≈ 20 s, 6 ≈ 12 s, 7 ≈ 25 s, 8 ≈ 20 s, 9 ≈ 8 s. Don't pad either: a story that's done at 90 s ends at 90 s. The hook is beat 1, and the town's rule IS the bizarre statement.

**Title formula:** `In This Town, [Rule in 3-6 words] [1 emoji]`, with one key word in CAPS (e.g. "In This Town, Shadows Are SOLD 🌑"). Score it with title.py as usual.

## Premise generator (mix and match, never copy a reference)
- **A thing everyone has** (shadows, birthdays, reflections, umbrellas, names) + **a strange rule** (can be sold, expires, swaps at midnight, grows when ___).
- **An ordinary act made dangerous** (humming, sneezing, saying "goodbye", looking up, eating bread after dark).
- **Horror engines:** something copies people; something only moves when unwatched; the town's protector is the threat; a sound/visitor that must never be answered; the rule exists because it already happened once; the narrator is the thing the town fears.
- **A hidden truth** for the twist: the rule protects the creature from us; the rule was made up by someone greedy; everyone breaks it secretly; the "punishment" is a gift.
